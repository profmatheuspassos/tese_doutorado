Chapter 5

The tools that already exist (and one that’s missing)

Title in the dissertationA participação cívica como impulso à democracia representativaTranslationCivic participation as a boost to representative democracy

Portugal and Brazil are representative democracies: citizens elect those who govern on their behalf. But they are also semi-direct democracies: besides voting, citizens have, on paper, tools to act on their own.

There are four: the referendum, the citizens’ initiative, the petition, and the citizen lawsuit. This chapter asks whether each one gives citizens an active role. Then it presents a fifth, which is missing in both countries.

As for the constitutions, Portugal’s goes much further. Brazil’s mentions the tools and leaves almost everything to legislation.

Referendum: it only happens if Parliament wants one

In a referendum, the population votes yes or no on an issue.

In Portugal, citizens can propose a referendum with 60,000 signatures. But the proposal has to get through three doors. The Assembly of the Republic, Portugal’s parliament, has to approve it, and the Constitutional Court checks whether it respects the constitution. Then the President of the Republic can refuse to call it for political reasons. And the result is only binding if more than half of registered voters take part.

In Brazil, citizens cannot even propose one. Only the National Congress calls the public vote.

Exercising power means getting others to act the way you want. If Parliament is the one that decides whether to hold the vote, citizens exercise no power. A referendum only happens if Parliament wants one.

These barriers exist, it is said, to prevent populism and manipulation. The author replies that checks and balances already exist, and proposes filtering instead of blocking. Citizens would propose the vote with a minimum number of signatures. The proposal would go straight to the court that guards the constitution, for a prior review. Anything that violated fundamental rights would stop there, without depending on the will of a politician.

Almost never used

In Portugal, from 1975 to 2019, there were eight referendums: three national (two in 1998 and one in 2007) and five local. In Brazil, from 1988 to 2019, there were five public votes: two national (1993 and 2005) and three local.

Practice is lacking. And the referendum suffers from the same ills as elections: distrust, lack of interest, and little connection with what is at stake. Those who no longer get excited about elections are unlikely to get excited about a rare public vote that seems like just another election.

A problem cannot be solved with the same principles that caused it. On its own, the referendum does not revitalize democracy. Besides changing the rules, citizens need to trust again. And that depends on them feeling part of a community.

Citizens’ initiative: the bill that changes hands

In a citizens’ initiative, citizens gather signatures and submit a bill.

In Portugal, 20,000 signatures are enough, and they can be sent online. A group of citizens represents the authors and is heard by Parliament.

In Brazil, it takes 1% of the electorate, in at least five states. Based on the 2018 electorate, that meant 1,473,063 signatures, all on paper, in a country 92 times larger than Portugal.

From 1988 to 2019, four federal laws came from bills proposed by citizens. But the Chamber of Deputies, the lower house of Congress, says it lacks the means to check signatures. So a deputy “sponsors” the bill, which moves forward as if it were the deputy’s own. Formally, no law was passed in Brazil as a citizens’ initiative. And the texts can be distorted along the way.

In Portugal, from 2003 to 2019, nine initiatives reached Parliament. Four became law.

The problems add up: signatures that are hard to get, lack of practice, and a middleman. The tool loses prestige and is rarely used. Voters think the bill will be ignored; elected officials think voters are not prepared to propose laws.

The author suggests checking signatures digitally, opening up more topics, and recognizing citizens as the authors. But the underlying cause is something else: those who don’t feel connected to others see no reason to work together.

Petition and citizen lawsuit

A petition is a request, a proposal, or a complaint to public authorities. It is free and requires no particular format. It does not guarantee that the request will be granted, but it gives the right to a reply.

In a citizen lawsuit, a citizen goes to court to defend what belongs to everyone, such as public property or the environment.

In Portugal, both tools are rights of political participation, and the petition has its own law. In Brazil, they are among individual rights. The petition has no law of its own, and the citizen lawsuit follows a 1965 law.

What petitions reveal

In Portugal, the share of citizens who had signed a petition went from 5.4%, between 2003 and 2011, to 21% in 2016.

A 2017 study shows more: two-thirds of petitions are collective, and 91.5% pursue a common interest. The number grew even at the height of the economic crisis, between 2010 and 2012.

Those who petition show trust. In the same study, 93.7% expected their request to be handled properly, and 84.3% expected to be heard by Parliament. Trust depends on how the request is handled, not on whether it is granted. Being heard counts for more than having the request granted.

This leads to some of the dissertation’s central conclusions. The disenchantment is with elections, not with participation. Citizens want to participate, and they do when there is an opportunity and when it has an effect. And they act together, in everyone’s interest, when they find a channel.

Saying “the right is there; anyone who wants it can go and find it” is an indirect incentive to apathy. These tools need to be actively promoted.

And in Brazil?

In surveys, the share of Brazilians who say they have signed a petition is even higher than in Portugal: it was 25.4% in 2015. But measuring actual use is difficult in a country with 5,570 municipalities.

One gauge is the Access to Information Law, from 2011, which allows anyone to request data from public authorities. Requests to the federal government rose from 102,423 (2015) to 129,309 (2018).

It sounds like a lot, but it isn’t. In 2018, these requests amounted to 0.088% of the electorate. For the Chamber of Deputies, 0.012%. And only 19% of requests to the Chamber came from people aged 30 or under. On this point, Portuguese involvement seems much greater, and Portugal is once again an example.

The missing tool: the recall

The recall allows voters to remove an elected official from office before the end of their term, through signatures and a vote.

It is not impeachment. Impeachment depends on the institutions and requires an impeachable offense. The recall starts with citizens and can have a political motive, such as poor management.

It exists in countries such as the United States, Switzerland, Canada, and Germany, but not in Portugal or Brazil. The author chooses the California model, not the Swiss one. In Switzerland, the recall is “dormant”: it has succeeded only once, in 1862.

In California, it works like this:

  1. At least ten voters publish a notice of intention, stating their reasons. The official has seven days to respond.
  2. Signature gathering begins. For state offices, the law requires 12% to 20% of the votes cast in the last election for that office. For local offices, 10% to 30% of registered voters.
  3. The elections office checks the signatures.
  4. The vote has two questions. Should the official leave office? That takes more than half of the votes. Who replaces them? The candidate with the most votes wins, and the official cannot run.

A recall is not allowed in the first 90 days or the last six months of the term.

From 1913 to 2019, there were 172 recall attempts against state officeholders in California. Only 9 made it to the ballot, and 6 removed the official from office.

In more than a century, there has been no revolution, no institutional breakdown, and no attack on the rights of those who lost. And the recall even increased trust in government in the long run.

The criticisms and the answers

Critics say the recall leads officials to think only about the short term and to fear hard decisions. It would drive away the most capable, divide society, confuse voters, cost a lot, and create yet another vote.

Supporters reply that officials are accountable all the time. Campaign donors carry less weight. Citizens follow politics more closely. And a crisis of legitimacy gets a peaceful way out, within the rules.

Impeachment and punishment by colleagues are not enough. They are legal processes, not political ones. Colleagues do not always want to punish one of their own. And neither of the two involves citizens.

Why the recall matters

In the dissertation’s view, the recall embodies active citizenship. No one carries out a recall alone. It means joining with others, seeing a common problem, gathering signatures, persuading other people, and answering for the result. That way, the vote comes from a considered choice, not from an impulse.

Even when the official stays, citizens learn that acting together changes the situation. Each one stops being, in the author’s words, “a passive person waiting for the next election.”

This is not an imperative mandate, in which the official merely follows orders. It is a channel, set out in law, for the conversation between voters and officials to continue after the election.

Adoption would be gradual: first, make room for it in the constitution; then, regulate it, especially at the local level, where active citizenship works best.

A complement, with limits

None of these tools replaces representative democracy. They complement it and can bring citizens closer to politics.

The author admits a weak point: they can open the door to political adventurism. It is seductive to tell voters that they, and not “the politicians,” are in control. That is why the majority must act within the law and take the minority into account. And the courts need to review the proposals, so that passions of the moment do not destroy what has been built.

Elections remain essential, but they aren’t enough. Authoritarian countries hold votes too. In a democracy, the will of citizens needs to have a real political effect and guide those who govern. Today, many people vote only to choose “the least bad.” The goal is to create the habit of participating.

Without equality, there is no democracy

Without economic and social equality, there is no democracy.

An ideal democracy would have five principles: free elections; separation of powers; free debate; direct participation, like participatory budgeting; and equality, including economic and social. The last two are usually forgotten in analyses.

Part of the disenchantment comes from this: democracy has not overcome exclusion. And wealth skews politics. Those with more money fund campaigns and pressure groups. They also negotiate from a position of strength, control the agenda of the debate, and tend to have more education.

Even when imperfect, democracy carries the seed of its own regeneration: it can correct its mistakes. And it needs to be defended. To that end, it is worth making more use of direct participation tools, especially at the local level. And it is worth remembering that rules are a necessary condition, but not a sufficient one, for democracy to exist.
