# The shortcut that doesn't fix it

In the face of a tired democracy, a chorus is growing: power is no longer in the people's hands,
and citizens have lost their voice. Dissatisfaction takes to the streets, in protests, and to the
polls, when leaders with radical rhetoric win elections.

From this comes a tempting idea: if technology is everywhere, why not decide everything directly,
over the internet? This is what is known as digital democracy. This chapter shows why it doesn't
fix the problem and may even make it worse.

<!-- B1 | Atenas | tradução de pt-br/capitulo-3.md, B1 -->

## Athens: the myth and the reality

Supporters of direct democracy usually think of ancient Athens, where everyone supposedly decided
everything in an assembly. It wasn't quite like that.

In the 4th century BC, Athens had about 30,000 citizens: only free men over the age of 20. Women,
children, foreigners, and slaves were left out.

The assembly met at least 40 times a year. On average, about 6,000 people attended, around 20% of
the citizens. Most only listened and voted. Few had the courage to speak.

Much of the power lay with smaller bodies, filled by lot from among volunteers. Of the roughly 700
magistrate posts, which handled the administration, about 600 were filled by lot, with a one-year
term. The Council had 500 members chosen by lot. Key posts, however, such as generals and finance
officials, were elected.

At the end of their term, magistrates had to account for their work. During the term, any citizen
could ask for their removal. And many were paid to take part, so that everyone would be on an equal
footing.

What defined Athens was rotation. About half of the citizens served on one of these bodies. Drawing
lots did not decide whether a person would hold office, but when.

The lesson is twofold. Athens was not as direct as people think. And the difference from today does
not lie in the number of people who exercise power, which has always been small. It lies in the
method of selection: there, drawing lots; here, elections.

## Why direct democracy doesn't fit today

Today's countries have millions of people. Bringing everyone together, all the time, to solve
everyday problems is impossible. If only some citizens showed up in Athens, absence would be even
greater with millions. And the more people there are, the less chance each one has to speak. Many give up,
and an organized minority takes control. Besides, it is hard for anyone to know a lot about every
public issue.

Hence the conclusion that direct democracy is unworkable in practice. Changing the way decisions
are made does not, by itself, fulfill the will of the people. And in the model imagined by digital
democracy, the will of the minority ends up excluded.

## Two alternatives, two limits

Participatory democracy creates channels, beyond voting, to hear from citizens between elections.
One example is participatory budgeting, in which residents help define the city's spending. The
Brazilian city of Porto Alegre has used the model since 1989. In Lisbon, citizens have been putting
forward proposals and voting on them since 2008, and the winning ones go into the following year's
budget.

The gain is that it teaches people about politics and brings voters and elected officials closer.
The limit is that, in practice, those who take part are mostly large organizations, able to
mobilize many people. Each person speaks only within their own group. And ordinary citizens, on
their own, remain far from the decision.

Deliberative democracy relies on dialogue: citizens and representatives debate, and only then is a
decision made. Social media would be the ideal place for this. But turning dialogue into a rule
creates bureaucracy, which pushes people away. And there is a lack of practical proposals for
including the less well-off and ensuring equality among those who debate. It is an interesting
idea, perhaps ideal, but impractical today.

The dissertation's proposal is not the same as either of the two. It seeks to restore the bonds
between people, so that representative democracy itself can work again.

## The digital promise

The internet evolved from the websites of the 1990s to social media, cell phones, and almost
tailor-made content, chosen by artificial intelligence.

For supporters of digital democracy, this changes everything. Anyone can speak. With more
information, people become more aware. Everyone monitors politicians. Leaders speak directly to the
people, in livestreams. And votes can be held over the internet.

It sounds promising. But technology cannot be seen as the savior of the nation.

## What happens in practice

**Information is poor.** False or distorted news circulates with eye-catching headlines. Most people
read only the headline and share it.

**A like is not a debate.** On social media, success is measured in likes, and each like spreads the
message. Besides, anyone can pay for it to reach millions of people. Many messages are made to
provoke an immediate reaction, such as "scandals" of every kind. Many politicians use the same
mechanism, and followers pass everything on, thinking they are playing a civic role.

**There is no conversation.** In the comments, supporters praise and opponents attack rudely. What
matters is who is speaking, not what they say.

**Surveillance becomes easier.** Dictatorships use the same tools to find opponents.

**Politicians cheapen themselves.** To seem like "one of us," they get into heated arguments and
reproduce the logic of "us versus them."

## The bubble

The internet was supposed to open each person up to many sources. In practice, it locks people into
bubbles.

Imagine someone who likes vegetarian cooking and searches for recipes. Over time, social media
learns about this interest and starts showing only vegetarian recipes. The person stops seeing, for
example, a recipe with meat that they could adapt using tofu.

The same happens in politics. The algorithm, the program that chooses what appears on the screen,
learns whether the person reads more left-wing or right-wing news. The side doesn't matter: they
will hardly see anything different. They are satisfied, because they only get what they like. So
is social media, because the person stays connected.

<!-- B7 | Nem todos estão conectados | tradução de pt-br/capitulo-3.md, B7 -->

## Not everyone is connected

Deciding everything over the internet requires everyone to have access. In Portugal, 63.9% of
people aged 16 to 74 used the internet in the first three months of 2018. In Brazil, in 2017, 74.8%
of the population lived in homes with access. In families with an income below $5.50 per person
per day, the figure was 58.3%. And the device and the internet plan still strain many people's
budgets.

Suppose everyone were connected. Would people want to take part in everything, all the time? Who
would hold public office, and how would they be chosen? And who would stop the majority of the
moment from overriding the constitution? Without answers to these questions, digital democracy
cannot take the place of representation.

## Seven problems, no solution

There are seven problems often identified in representative democracy. The question here is not
whether they are real, only whether digital democracy would fix them.

A clarification about the first one. A law that punishes crimes goes against the will of those who
commit them, and that is not tyranny. There is tyranny of the majority when it persecutes the
opposition, denies basic freedoms, or eliminates the checks on power.

| Problem | Does digital democracy fix it? |
|---|---|
| A majority that crushes the minority | No. Voting directly gives the feeling that one's own will must be carried out at any cost. |
| Culture and values dumbed down | No; it makes it worse, with poor information and a false sense of anonymity. |
| Ineffective government | No. Each citizen becomes a center of power, and it gets harder to reach agreements. |
| Conflict between groups | No. The internet encourages "us versus them." |
| Demagogues speaking "on behalf of the people" | No. "The people" is no one in particular, and it is on the internet that the right to speak for it is fought over. |
| Silent exclusion by race, class, or gender | No guarantee. Economic barriers still carry weight. |
| Irrational decisions | No. Irrationality lies in people's choices, not in the medium used. |

## When the opponent becomes an enemy

In the dissertation's view, the central problem is fragmentation: people stop seeing themselves as
part of a whole. Digital democracy deepens this problem. Each person gets only what is, supposedly,
good for them. And decisions are made "on the spot," with no time to think about others.

The competition for power is healthy, but it needs limits. Without them, the opponent becomes an
enemy. With an opponent, you can negotiate. An enemy "must be destroyed": defeated or ignored, as
if they did not exist.

People cannot be expected to be altruistic all the time. That is why institutional mechanisms are
needed to help them seek agreement.

## Populism

Social media has become the main channel between elected officials and voters. A rumor spreads with
a single click, and the correction, when it comes, has a much smaller effect. In this environment,
populism gained strength, especially in the second half of the 2010s. This is true of new and old
democracies. And it is not just leaders: there are also populations that support it.

Populism is not an ideology. It is a political formula, on the left or on the right. At its center
is "the people," treated as a pure group that holds every virtue. The leader almost never says
clearly who these people are, and so each listener feels part of them. "The people" represents
everyone and, for that reason, no one. On the other side is an elite described as corrupt and
serving outside interests. That is the enemy.

The leader presents themselves as the only one able to see the truth and is always campaigning.
They treat parties, parliament, and the courts as obstacles and prefer to govern by decree. They
use public votes not so that the people can decide, but as an endorsement of the leader personally.

In the dissertation's view, populism is a degeneration of democracy. The leader puts themselves
above the law, and their decisions become impossible to question. Participating stops being about
deciding and becomes blindly supporting the leader. Each citizen becomes just one more pawn in the
crowd.

## Something more is needed

Voting every day, instead of every four years, increases the quantity of participation, not its
quality. It also doesn't make people trust institutions or each other more.

The internet does have a good side: it is an informal space for debate. But it is used more to
spread ideas than to decide together. And digital democracy does not keep the promise of direct
democracy, nor that of participatory democracy, nor that of deliberative democracy. It can
complement representation, not replace it. The most useful role of digital media is to spread the
values of a democratic culture.

Something more is needed. The dissertation points to four paths:

- creating real bonds between people and the community where they live;
- understanding citizenship as action, and not just as the right to vote;
- democratizing parties from the inside;
- using direct participation tools that truly complement representation.

The first three are the subject of the next chapter. The fourth is left for the last one.
