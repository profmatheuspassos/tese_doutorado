Chapter 1

On paper, everything is fine

Title in the dissertationA construção conceitual e normativa da democracia e da cidadania em Portugal e no BrasilTranslationThe conceptual and normative construction of democracy and citizenship in Portugal and Brazil

To say that a democracy is doing badly, you first need to know what to expect from it. This chapter builds that yardstick. It explains what representative democracy is and what it means to be a citizen. Then it checks whether the laws of Portugal and Brazil have what the yardstick calls for.

The answer is yes. Both countries are democracies on paper. The open question is whether this reaches real life.

From subject to citizen

Subject
Representative government
Representative democracy

In ancient Athens, citizens could take part directly in the city’s decisions. In a country with millions of people, this is not possible. That is why today’s democracies are representative: the people elect individuals who decide on their behalf.

In the absolutist State, people were subjects of the ruler. In 1789, the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen said something else: the law expresses the will of the citizens.

But democracy did not come right away. Between the late 18th century and the early 20th century, there was what is known as representative government. There were elections, but with two differences from today.

First, few people voted. As a rule, women were left out, and many countries required a minimum income to vote. In 19th-century England, it is estimated that voters made up only about 10% of the population.

Second, elections did not create a bond of representation. They served to choose the “notables,” the big names of the elite. Elected officials depended on personal connections, but voted as they pleased, with no commitment to voters.

The turning point came in the late 19th century and the early 20th century, also as a result of World War I. The vote became a real power for voters. The law began to link elected officials and voters. Those who were dissatisfied could replace the elected official in the next election.

At the same time, the vote spread. The organization of workers, the vote as a reward for those who survived the wars, and the decline in illiteracy all played a part. In a little over a hundred years, almost all citizens began to vote.

Why parties became the centerpiece

In today’s democracy, voters choose mainly among parties. That is why people speak of party democracy. Labor unions and nongovernmental organizations try to influence the government. The party, by contrast, is the only organization that the constitution allows to enter the State to act on behalf of citizens.

Every party, of any ideology, has one goal above all others: winning votes. In the 19th century, parties existed almost exclusively inside parliament. Once the vote expanded, they became mass parties, with a permanent structure and year-round campaigning.

This changed voting. Voters stopped voting for the person and started voting for the party’s platform. As a result, elected officials began to follow the party line, and the party needs to show voters that it keeps its promises. Parties also began to provide support for the government, often through alliances.

A majority is a count, not the truth

Democracy assumes a pluralistic society, in which no group smothers the others. In this view, a majority is just a way of counting votes. Winning an election does not prove that an idea is true or fair. Imagine a group that wins with 55% of the votes. It gains the right to govern, but not the right to take away the basic rights of those who lost. Today’s majority may be tomorrow’s minority.

When the majority thinks it can do anything, the “tyranny of the majority” arises: authoritarianism disguised as democracy.

Nor can citizens limit themselves to voting. The dissertation speaks of a duty to take part in political life, broader than voting. Between one election and the next, there are tools to monitor those who govern, and they deserve encouragement. A referendum puts a question to a public vote. A petition is a formal request to the authorities. A citizen lawsuit is a court case, brought by a citizen, to protect what belongs to everyone, such as public property and the environment.

The nine rules of the game

Political theory sums up democracy as a set of rules. There are nine:

PortugalBrazil
  1. Parliament is elected by the people.
  2. Other offices are also elected, such as the head of state and local governments.
  3. All adults vote, regardless of race, religion, income, or sex.
  4. Every vote has the same weight.
  5. Each voter forms and expresses their own opinion freely.
  6. There is a real choice, with at least two parties.
  7. Majority rule applies, in elections and in votes in parliament.
  8. The majority cannot take away the rights of the minority, or the minority’s right to become the majority one day.
  9. The government depends on the confidence of parliament or of a president elected by the people.

Rules are not enough. A democratic government also needs to respond to citizens and be accountable to them. People need to be able to form their preferences, express them, and see the government take them into account, without discrimination. So a country is not simply democratic or not: it can be more or less democratic.

On paper, Portugal and Brazil follow the nine rules, as the following sections show.

Two ways to be a citizen

With the Declaration of 1789, people stopped being subjects and began to have rights. Since then, the word “citizen” has had two meanings.

In the broad sense, a citizen is every human being, just by existing. They have the right to a dignified life, with all the rights that support it. This does not depend on nationality.

In the narrow sense, a citizen is someone who can exercise political rights, that is, vote and run for office. To do so, you have to register as a voter.

An example from Brazil. A 15-year-old is a citizen in the broad sense, but not yet in the narrow one. A 25-year-old adult is a citizen in both senses. This does not make the adult more of a citizen. The adult can simply exercise more rights.

Today’s democracy rests on the narrow sense. But the other rights also count: without freedom of expression and without a basic education, no one participates well. Democracy and citizenship go together.

What Portugal’s constitution says

The constitution is the most important law in a country. Portugal’s dates from 1976 and came after a dictatorship. It bases the State on the will of the people and on human dignity.

The text goes beyond elections. It says that it is up to the State “to ensure and encourage the democratic participation of citizens.” And it calls for real equality among people, not just equality before the law.

Voting is both a right and a civic duty. The rights are called “civic and political,” not just political. Parties must have internal democracy, with the participation of all members. Chapter 2 shows that, in practice, this does not happen. And citizens can propose a referendum to parliament.

Local government also has its doors open. In local elections, groups of citizens, and not just parties, can put forward candidates. Voters can request local referendums. And in small parishes, the law can replace the elected assembly with a meeting of all voters. The parish is the smallest division of local government in Portugal.

What Brazil’s constitution says

Brazil’s constitution dates from 1988. Right at the start, it places citizenship, human dignity, and political pluralism among the country’s foundations. And it states: “All power comes from the people, who exercise it through elected representatives or directly.”

The order of the words matters. Representatives come first; direct participation comes only after. The constitution makes clear its preference for representative democracy.

Besides freedoms, the petition, and the citizen lawsuit, the people can decide by plebiscite and referendum. And they can submit bills, known as the citizens’ initiative.

Voting is mandatory for most adults. The fine for not voting is low: R$3.51 (Brazilian reais), the amount cited in 2019. The weight lies in the other penalties. Anyone who fails to vote and does not settle the matter cannot get a passport, for example. They also cannot be sworn in to a post won in a civil service exam.

In 2006, a change to the constitution let parties form different alliances in each race, with no consistency between the national, state, and municipal levels. In the author’s view, this helped parties multiply. In 2017, another change banned alliances in elections for deputies and city council members. And it created the electoral threshold. A party that does not reach a minimum number of votes or of elected federal deputies loses its free radio and TV airtime. It also loses the party fund, the money that the electoral courts pass on to parties. The rule gets stricter with each election, until 2030.

Calendar Presidential and parliamentary elections never coincide The president, governors, senators, and deputies are elected on the same day

And in practice?

Both countries pass the test. They have the rules of the game and the principles that support them, such as freedom, equality, and citizenship. From the standpoint of their laws, they are democracies.

There is a difference in tone. The Portuguese constitution requires the State to pursue real equality and to encourage participation. The Brazilian one also provides for social rights, but it is vaguer on this point. And it speaks only of political rights, without the adjective “civic.”

The main question remains: does this democracy on paper become real democracy, in people’s everyday lives? The dissertation’s answer is no. The next chapter shows why.
