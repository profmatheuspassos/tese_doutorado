# Revitalize, not replace

This site presents, in plain language, the doctoral dissertation in Law by Matheus Passos Silva,
which compares Portugal and Brazil. Both countries have free elections, parties, and freedom of
expression. Even so, many people feel that their own voice doesn't count.

The way out is not to replace representative democracy, the system in which the people elect
individuals to decide on their behalf. It is to revitalize it, putting citizens at the center of
decisions about the place where they live.

<!-- B1 | Uma cena | tradução de pt-br/home.md, B1 -->

## A scene

In June 2013, protests against the bus fare increase took over the streets of São Paulo.
The protests spread to other cities.
Some of the demands were met: the fare came down.

At first glance, the scene proves that democracy works. There was freedom to protest, leaders
responded, and the country has free elections.

It's not enough. Having the right rules is necessary, but not sufficient. If it were enough,
Brazil under military rule, which had almost all of these rules, would have been a democracy.

<!-- B2 | No papel | tradução de pt-br/home.md, B2 -->

## On paper, everything is fine

Political theory brings together the so-called "rules of the game" of democracy. Among them:

- a parliament elected by the people;
- votes for all adults, regardless of race, religion, income, or sex;
- a vote with the same weight for everyone;
- freedom to form and express your own opinion;
- at least two parties, so there is a real choice;
- decisions made by the majority;
- a majority that cannot take away the rights of the minority, or the minority's right to become the majority one day.

A democratic government also needs to respond to citizens and be accountable to them.

Portugal and Brazil follow all these rules, both in the constitution, the most important law in
each country, and in the electoral laws. On paper, they are democracies. What remains to be seen
is whether this reaches everyday life.

<!-- B3 | Na prática | tradução de pt-br/home.md, B3 -->

## In practice, trust collapses

For most people, participating has come to mean just voting from time to time. In Brazil, being a
citizen came to mean, in practice, having a voter registration card. And many people don't even
vote, or they cast a blank vote or spoil their ballot. The reasoning fits in a single question: why
participate, if the result never changes?

In Brazil, in 2017, 92.9% of respondents said that most people cannot be trusted. Distrust also
reaches institutions, with a sharp drop in trust between 2010 and 2017. In that period, the share
of people with no trust at all:

- in parties went from 40.3% to 72.4%;
- in the government, from 16.1% to 66.1%;
- in the National Congress, from 20.4% to 60.2%.

Source for the Brazil figures: Latinobarómetro.

In Portugal, in November 2018, only 17% of respondents trusted the parties. The data come from the Portal de Opinião Pública of the Fundação Francisco Manuel dos Santos.

## A circle that feeds itself

Three problems reinforce each other.

**Citizens pull away.** Voting is not the same as getting involved. Those who vote for "just
anyone" because they think they're all the same have participated, but have not gotten involved.

**Parties close themselves off.** They are the main gateway to power and should be democratic on
the inside. In practice, a few party officials decide the platform and the candidates. In
Portugal, the law requires internal democracy, but practice falls short. In Brazil, the law
doesn't even require it explicitly.

**People stop seeing themselves as part of a whole.** This is called political fragmentation.
Everyone looks out for themselves, there are no shared projects, and the feeling grows that
nothing changes.

The more citizens pull away, the more parties close themselves off. The more they close themselves
off, the less citizens feel represented. Neither side has a strong enough incentive to change. In
the dissertation's view, the root lies in the institutions. A system that only calls on citizens
when it is time to vote does not encourage them to connect with others.

## The shortcut that doesn't fix it

Faced with this, the idea of digital democracy gained ground: instead of electing representatives,
people would decide directly, over the internet. It doesn't fix it.

**Not everyone is connected.** In 2018, 63.9% of Portuguese people aged 16 to 74 had used the
internet in the first three months of the year. In Brazil, in 2017, 74.8% of people lived in homes
with access.

**The internet rewards quick reactions, not debate.** Success is measured in likes. Algorithms show each person only what they already like. Someone who searches for vegetarian
recipes starts seeing only vegetarian recipes. In politics, everyone stays in their own bubble.

**The opponent becomes an enemy.** With an opponent, you can negotiate. An enemy "must be
destroyed." The digital world deepens the fragmentation.

**It makes room for populism.** Populism is a way of doing politics that pits a pure "people"
against an enemy "elite," with a leader who puts themselves above the rules. In the dissertation's
view, it is a degeneration of democracy.

Voting every day, instead of every four years, increases the quantity of participation, not its
quality. Technology can be a complement, but it is not salvation. Something more is needed.

## Citizens at the center

The proposal has three parts.

**1. Feeling part of the community.** The technical name is patriotic identification. The name
can be misleading: the idea is that people see themselves as responsible, together, for the
community where they live. It works best in the neighborhood or the city.

This bond doesn't depend on kindness, but on logic. Imagine five people deciding something that
affects all of them. If each one defends their own idea at any cost, there is no agreement. If
they realize that harm to the others ends up coming back to hurt them, they look for a solution
that is good enough for all of them.

**2. Active citizenship.** It means citizens deciding, together with others, the public policies
of the place where they live. Public policies are the actions public authorities take to solve
collective problems, such as street lighting. Those who decide together also answer for the result.

Today, everything revolves around the government, and citizens only watch. The proposal is
citizen-centered governance, in which citizens act alongside the State, not against it or in its
place. People pull away from politics because they feel powerless. When participation has an
effect, interest appears.

**3. More open parties.** People who get involved in neighborhood issues start turning to the
parties for bigger issues. So as not to lose these supporters, parties need to open up. The
vicious circle starts to turn the other way.

### Portugal: the branches are thin and need tending

In Portugal, the opening exists, but it is fragile. The constitution encourages participation. In
parishes, the smallest division of local government, the very small ones can replace the elected
assembly with a meeting of all voters. But this reaches only 0.22% of the population, based on the
2011 census. In the others, citizens can bring proposals, but they do not vote. And the assembly
only votes on a proposal if it wants to.

### Brazil: the seeds are planted, but the ground is arid

In Brazil, the situation is even less favorable. The constitution provides for 30 forms of
participation beyond voting, but none lets citizens directly decide a public policy. The law that
guides how cities are managed requires hearings and debates on the budget. Citizens are heard, but
they do not decide.

In both countries, the rules make room for citizens to be present, not to decide. The dissertation
proposes making this opening clear in the constitution and spelling out in the law how it works.
Culture does not change through the law alone, but it also changes through it.

## The tools that already exist (and one that's missing)

Besides voting, both countries already offer four tools:

- **Referendum** The population votes yes or no on an issue.
- **Citizens' initiative** Citizens gather signatures and submit a bill.
- **Petition** Anyone can make a request or a complaint to public authorities.
- **Citizen lawsuit** A citizen goes to court to overturn an act that harms what belongs to everyone.

The referendum and the citizens' initiative are almost never used and depend too much on elected
officials. A referendum only happens if Parliament wants one.

Both countries are missing one tool: the recall. With it, voters gather signatures and then vote on
whether an elected official should leave office before the end of their term. The state of
California, in the United States, uses the recall. The suggestion is to adopt it gradually,
starting at the local level.

All these tools complement representation; they do not replace it. And they can be used for
political adventurism. That is why they need limits in the law, respect for the minority, and
oversight by the courts.

## The conclusions

**Elections are essential, but they aren't enough.** When about half of voters turn out, a leader
can get elected with the support of just a quarter of them.

**Citizens are not uninterested.** Look at the petitions to the Portuguese Parliament. In 2016,
21% of Portuguese people said they had signed a petition. Between 2003 and 2011, it was 5.4%.
Two-thirds of petitions are collective, and 91.5% pursue a common interest.

**Being heard matters a lot.** In a survey, 93.7% of people who had sent petitions expected proper
treatment, and 84.3% expected to be heard. Trust depends more on being heard than on seeing the
request granted. The disenchantment is with elections, not with participation.

**Interest is local.** People feel better able to solve, with others, the problems close to them.

**Portugal is ahead of Brazil on the rules**, but it also has flaws and can move forward.

### Seven problems, two answers

See how the two ways out deal with seven problems identified in representative democracy.

| Problem | Does the digital world fix it? | The answer from active citizenship |
|---|---|---|
| A majority that crushes the minority | No | Local decisions, in diverse groups, keep one group from dominating |
| Culture and values dumbed down | No; it makes it worse | Those who take part learn about the neighborhood and the city |
| Ineffective government | No | Resources go to real needs, and those who decide feel responsible |
| Conflict between groups | No; it encourages "us versus them" | Conflicts remain, but people seek agreement, not total victory |
| Demagogues speaking "on behalf of the people" | No | Citizens' action between elections fills that space |
| Silent exclusion by race, class, or gender | No guarantee | Bottom-up participation helps reduce it |
| Irrational decisions | No | Those who live with the problem take part in the decision |

### No magic formula

None of this depends on altruism. Changing the law doesn't make anyone participate overnight.
What exists is a possibility: with real opportunities, people participate. And when they are at
least heard, they start to take an interest in what belongs to everyone.
