Chapter 4

Citizens at the center

Title in the dissertationA revalorização da democracia representativa por meio da atribuição ao cidadão de uma posição central no processo jurídico-políticoTranslationRevaluing representative democracy by giving citizens a central position in the legal and political process

Representative democracy was designed in the late 19th century and has barely changed in a hundred years. Society has. We live in the 21st century with a model from another time.

Those who feel no connection with elected officials think that holding them accountable is a waste of time. They conclude that democracy doesn’t solve their problems and become more open to populist adventurism.

The proposed way out has three parts: the bond with the community, active citizenship, and parties that are democratic on the inside. None of them replaces elections, parties, or the State. The goal is to revitalize representation, not to replace it.

Feeling part of the community

The technical name is patriotic identification, but it has nothing to do with flags. It means each person seeing themselves, together with others, as responsible for the community where they live.

It is worth saying what it is not. No one is required to participate or to care about others. It does not require frequent meetings or create parallel institutions. It does not expect people to be kind. It is not the will of the majority at any cost: having more votes does not make a decision good.

Rights, elections, and access to the courts stay the same. Today, politics separates “us,” the voters, from “them,” the elected officials. The idea is that the feeling of belonging gradually replaces the feeling of isolation. Across a whole country, this is difficult. In the neighborhood or the parish, the smallest division of local government in Portugal, it becomes possible.

Logic, not kindness

Imagine five people who need to decide something that affects all of them. If each one defends their own proposal at any cost, there is no agreement. If they realize that harm to the others ends up coming back to hurt them, they look for a solution that is good enough for all of them. It is a rational calculation, not a moral one.

That is why the law can help. Imposing kindness would be typical of a closed regime. But the law can encourage behavior. That is what it has done for the last hundred years, by teaching that participating means voting.

From passive to active citizenship

In traditional citizenship, citizens have rights but almost no duties toward others. Their contact with public life is the election. After that, they watch.

In active citizenship, citizens organize themselves and, together with public authorities, decide on public policies that affect their daily lives. Public policies are the actions public authorities take to solve collective problems. Citizens act alongside the State, not against it or in its place. And those who decide together answer for it together.

This calls for another model of governance, the way the State and society organize themselves to make decisions. In government-centered governance, the current model, citizens vote and then only elected officials decide. If something goes wrong, it is easy to blame “them.” In citizen-centered governance, those affected by a policy can take part in creating it, with real decision-making power. They stop being observers and become actors. And they share responsibility for the result.

Close to home

Active citizenship does not mean taking an interest in everything. Citizens get involved with what is close to them. In general, they care more about the lighting on their street than about the exchange rate.

That is why the proposal calls for decentralization. Today, citizens feel that everything is decided “out there,” in the central government, by “them.” The idea is for decisions to be made “here,” by “us.” Close to home, the machinery of the State is less intimidating, and it is easier to demand results. For matters far removed from everyday life, elections are still necessary.

Apathetic because powerless

A common objection: if many people don’t even vote, why would they take part in something that takes more effort?

Because today there are few forms of participation that actually work. The paths that exist, such as a formal request to public authorities or a bill backed by signatures, are bureaucratic. And the result is uncertain: the proposal may be rejected or approved with different content. Citizens give up, and all that is left is the election, which no longer interests them.

It is a rational choice. People are apathetic because they feel powerless, not the other way around.

An example that already exists

Italy’s constitution requires all levels of government to support citizens who take the initiative, alone or in groups, in activities of general interest. The State does not give up its own duties. It only recognizes that some actions can be better designed and carried out by citizens themselves.

Portugal: the branches are thin and need tending

The Portuguese constitution requires the State to encourage participation. It treats protecting the environment as a duty of citizens as well. But it does not say on which issues or how. It leaves that to the law, which can expand or restrict it.

Residents’ organizations can have representatives in the parish assembly, but without a vote. And participating without voting is the same as not participating.

0.22%

In parishes with up to 150 voters, the elected assembly gives way to a meeting of all voters. By the author’s calculations, based on the 2011 census, there are only 129 of these parishes, with 23,466 inhabitants: 0.22% of the population.

In the others, citizens can gather signatures and request a special session of the assembly. Two representatives present proposals. But they do not vote, and the assembly only votes on the proposal if it wants to.

Sessions are public, with time set aside for the public. But no citizen may interfere in the discussions, applaud, or express disapproval. The law, from 2013, provides for a fine of 150 to 750 euros.

The result is a snowball effect: if my proposals are not even considered locally, what chance do they have nationally? The dissertation does not ask for every proposal to be approved. It asks that each one at least be considered.

Brazil: the seeds are planted, but the ground is arid

In Brazil, the situation is even less favorable. The constitution provides for 30 forms of participation beyond voting, according to one study. None of them lets citizens take part directly in creating a public policy. In the part on municipalities, the only door is the citizens’ initiative for local laws, with the support of 5% of voters.

Each municipality has its own charter, and there are more than 5,500 of them. That is why the analysis uses the law that guides urban policy in every city, the City Statute. It calls for participation at every stage. It requires debates and hearings on the budget before the city council approves it. And it requires the master plan, which defines how the city grows, to be discussed in public.

But participation is much more advisory than decision-making. Hearings are used more by leaders to account for their work than by citizens to present ideas. And the law does not say what the role of each group is. In both countries, citizens can listen, reflect, and get informed. The final say belongs only to public authorities.

Even so, in the author’s view, Portugal is much further ahead. There, participation is at least in the constitution, and small parishes govern themselves. Brazil should, once again, follow Portugal’s example.

Being present is not deciding

In both countries, the rules make room for citizens to be present, not to decide. At local meetings, those already inside block anything new, and those outside only get in with great difficulty.

There is also the prejudice that ordinary citizens are uneducated and should stay out. It is 19th-century reasoning, from when only those with an income voted. Democracy is a process, with ups and downs, and it is not up to the “enlightened” to act as guardians of everyone else.

The dissertation proposes three changes:

  • In the constitution: say clearly that citizens can help build public policies, especially local ones.
  • In the law: say how this works in practice. Vague phrases, such as “participation is encouraged,” are not enough.
  • In culture: the law is not a magic solution. Culture does not change through the law alone, but it also changes through it.

Personal data protection shows how the law changes habits. The idea was already decades old, and a European directive had existed since 1995. But it only became everyone’s concern with the European regulation, the GDPR, approved in 2016. People learned about their rights, companies had to adapt, and other countries, Brazil among them, passed their own laws.

A problem cannot be solved with what caused it. Strengthening only today’s channels of representation is not enough to revitalize democracy.

Parties that are democratic on the inside

Both constitutions guarantee autonomy to parties, as a reaction to the authoritarian regimes that came before. In Portugal, the constitution and the law require internal democracy: bodies elected by party members and positions that cannot be held for life. In Brazil, neither the constitution nor the law makes this requirement explicitly.

In 2019, a change to Brazilian law allowed parties’ provisional leadership to last up to eight years. In the dissertation’s view, it is an “extremely undemocratic” rule. In June 2019, in 26 of 35 parties, more than half of the structure consisted of this kind of leadership, called provisional committees. Their officials are appointed, not elected. They are concentrated in the municipalities, where the party is closest to people.

It is these party officials who manage the party’s money, choose candidates, and decide on alliances. The leaders end up controlling who votes for them.

From a vicious to a virtuous circle

Parties close themselves off for three reasons, and each one has a way out.

Too much autonomy. Parties, especially in Brazil, do not practice democracy on the inside, and party conventions become events for the press. The way out is a law that requires internal democracy, transparency, and the renewal of party officials, inspired by Portugal’s. The law alone is not enough, but it is necessary.

Isolation. Parties only reach out to people at election time. But they have duties toward citizens, not just toward voters: to inform them and educate them politically. With active citizenship, politics becomes an ongoing process, and the two sides grow closer.

Passive citizens. Passivity feeds the parties’ isolation. Those who get involved in neighborhood issues relearn how to do politics. For distant issues, they tend to turn to the party. And parties, so as not to lose these supporters, need to open up.

This is how the vicious circle starts to turn the other way.

The objections

The author anticipates the criticisms. Elected officials would not give up power. The current model is a hundred years old and has brought growth, so there would be no alternative.

But dissatisfaction with democracy does not depend only on the economy. We are living through a transition in the very meaning of the word democracy, and its outcome is still uncertain. The author calls these proposals “ideas that reveal unease” with the way things are.

What remains is to look at the direct participation tools that already exist, and at one that is missing. That is the subject of the last chapter.
