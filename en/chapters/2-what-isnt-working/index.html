Chapter 2

What isn’t working

Title in the dissertationAs disfunções da democracia representativa no século XXITranslationThe dysfunctions of representative democracy in the 21st century

The previous chapter showed that Portugal and Brazil are democracies on paper. But rules are only half the story. A strong constitution does no good if, on the other side, citizens don’t get involved.

This chapter points out three problems. Citizens lose interest in politics. Parties close themselves off. And society breaks into pieces that don’t see themselves as a whole. The three feed each other, in a vicious circle.

Citizens pull away

Parties close themselves off

Political fragmentation

Participating is not the same as getting involved

Imagine a person who goes to vote and picks just anyone, without giving it any thought, because they think “they’re all the same.” They participated, because they voted. But they did not get involved.

Participating goes beyond voting. Being active in a party, going to a demonstration, and even following the news also count. The dissertation goes further. Parents who go to school meetings can influence education policy. Residents who go to condo association meetings can have a say in a decision about water and sewage services.

Democracy calls for a sense of belonging to a collective. The people are the owners of power, and responsibility for what belongs to everyone is shared by everyone.

Three steps of participation

The first is being present. A person reads a news story, watches the news on TV, or goes to the condo association meeting. They take in what others produce, without contributing anything of their own.

The second is becoming active. A person works for a party, joins a campaign, or goes to a protest.

The third is deciding. Directly, only some hundreds or thousands of people decide, such as members of parliament, leaders, judges, mayors, and city council members. Indirectly, those who vote decide, because they choose who will govern.

In practice, most people stay on the first step and vote from time to time. The reasoning fits in a single question: why participate, if the result is never any different?

Portugal’s numbers

In Portugal, the data on trust come from the Portal de Opinião Pública of the Fundação Francisco Manuel dos Santos.

In May 2013, only 10% of respondents trusted the government, and 13% trusted parliament. The numbers rose afterward, but in November 2018 they stood at 43% and 37%.

For the parties, the situation is worse. Starting in November 2000, there were 34 surveys. In only seven of them did trust in the parties go above 20%. In November 2018, it was 17%. This is serious, because parties are supposed to represent citizens.

In elections for the Portuguese parliament, abstention, that is, the share of voters who did not turn out, was 36.91% in 1999. It fell to 32.80% in 2005, an outlier. Then it rose again and reached 40.42% in 2015.

In the 2019 European Parliament election, 69.27% of Portuguese voters did not turn out. A year earlier, in a European survey, Portuguese people gave reasons for not voting. For 59%, trust in the political system was lacking. For 54%, their vote would change nothing.

Brazil’s numbers

92.9%

In Brazil, the data come from Latinobarómetro, an opinion survey carried out in several countries. In 2017, only 6.8% of respondents said that most people can be trusted. Another 92.9% said no. Without mutual trust, political trust becomes even more fragile.

The survey also asked: “How much trust do you have in this institution?” The table shows how many answered “no trust at all.”

Institution 2006 2010 2017
National Congress 28.7% 20.4% 60.2%
Government 17.6% 16.1% 66.1%
Judiciary 16.8% 17.9% 37.2%
Superior Electoral Court 10.1% 17.9% 40.2%
Political parties 40.1% 40.3% 72.4%

Between 2006 and 2010, the numbers changed little in most cases. The sharp drop in trust came between 2010 and 2017.

At the polls, the numbers go up and down. In the presidential election, abstention was 16.77% in 2006 and 22.13% in 2010, the highest since 1994. In 2018, it was 20.33%, according to the electoral courts.

A perfect and empty election

Abstaining, casting a blank vote, or spoiling the ballot can be read as a protest against the system. In Portugal, turning out to vote is optional, and the protest shows up as abstention. In Brazil, voting is mandatory, and the protest shows up more in blank votes or spoiled ballots. In 2018, 17.88% of the votes for the Senate were spoiled.

Imagine a presidential election with two candidates. All voters abstain, cast a blank vote, or spoil their ballot, except three people. Candidate A, who is single, votes for himself. Candidate B votes for himself and brings his wife, who also votes for him.

B wins in the first round, with 67% of the valid votes. Under the law, there is nothing to challenge. Each voter exercised their rights as they wished. But it is hard to say that the will of the people was fulfilled.

The example shows the limits of the rules. They are necessary for democracy, but they are not enough.

Why people give up

There are three common ways to stop participating.

In the first, a person sees no channel for being heard. Representatives seem distant, sometimes even on the map, because they are in the capital.

In the second, the person tried. They voted for party A, and nothing changed in their life. They voted for B, then for C, and again nothing. Over time, the motivation fades. They stop voting, cast a blank vote, or spoil their ballot.

In the third, the person doesn’t know how the system works. They may want to bring a proposal to a party meeting, but don’t know where to start. So they leave politics “to others.” The lack of formal education plays a part here.

It is up to citizens to seek information, but it is up to the State to guarantee the minimum conditions for a dignified life. Political culture also carries weight. It revolves around the duty to vote and, in Brazil, around the penalties for those who don’t vote. The way out is to give participation a positive meaning, with democratic parties that pay attention to citizens and direct participation tools that work.

Parties closed in on themselves

Parties are the gateway to power. That is why they should be democratic on the inside. An authoritarian party in a democratic regime is a contradiction.

In Portugal, the constitution and the law require this clearly. All party members take part in electing the party’s bodies, positions must be renewed, and internal voting is secret.

In Brazil, the requirement is only implicit. The law on parties mentions democracy only two times. A 2019 change allowed a party’s provisional leadership to last up to eight years. In that time, the country holds four elections. In the author’s view, two years would be reasonable. Beyond that, the “provisional” leadership becomes permanent.

Even in Portugal, practice falls short of the law. Party bylaws are more about image than reality.

In general, parties have a majority of members who follow and a minority of professionals who decide. It is these few who define the campaign proposals and the list of candidates. Party officials who stay in office for a long time drift away from the base and look after their own interests. This is called oligarchization: the party comes to be run by a few.

Rival parties can also make quiet deals to protect themselves when electoral rules change. Yesterday’s fierce opponents become today’s allies. For voters, the choice starts to look like a smokescreen. And the blame does not lie only with the top leadership: many party members take no interest in the party’s internal life.

Politics became a ratings game

Radio, TV, and the internet bring candidates closer to voters, who see them as “one of us.” People vote for the person, not for the party’s platform. More and more, whoever does well in front of the cameras wins.

Proposals become vague. “More investment in education”: who is against it? Accountability also turns into media appearances. For voters, this is convenient, but it leaves them in a passive position.

From this personalization come the “saviors of the nation.” They exist on the right, on the left, and in the center. Loyalty goes to people, not to institutions, and parties grow weaker.

Those in power don’t want to lose privileges, and making room for popular participation becomes the last resort. Voters and parties become complacent. Direct participation tools appear now and then, on issues that don’t disturb what is already in place. They serve to create the impression that democracy works.

Everyone for themselves

Democracy has always depended on dedication to the common good. In Athens, individualism helped bring it down: many citizens only took part because of the payment they received.

The common good, here, is not kindness or altruism. It is knowing that today I can decide and tomorrow I may be affected by someone else’s decision. That is why those who decide tend to act with care and expect the same from others.

Today, people distrust one another and participate thinking of themselves and their families. In Portugal, distrust is lower than in Brazil. On a scale of 0 to 10, trust in people stood at 4.1 in December 2018.

The result is political fragmentation. People see themselves as loose pieces, not as part of a whole. They become less and less able to define a common goal and reach it. The feeling of powerlessness grows.

Voters think: “It’s not my vote that will decide the election.” And they vote thinking only of themselves. When everyone does this, representation becomes a vote count, with no political content. Those who get results are organized groups, such as labor unions and nongovernmental organizations. And the rule becomes “either my group wins everything, or it wins nothing.”

Two ways to see democracy

One view treats democracy only as a method for choosing who governs. Voters elect someone and, in the next election, keep or replace them. Between one election and the next, they should stay quiet, without even reaching out to the elected official to make suggestions. Anyone who is unhappy should run for office.

Another view treats democracy as something built together. It calls for three things. People see themselves as part of a community with shared goals. All groups are truly heard. And the outcome follows the majority, but includes the minority whenever possible.

This is not direct democracy. Citizens help define what should be done. Representatives still decide how to do it.

The constitutions of Portugal and Brazil are closer to the first view. Neither follows it to the letter, but neither creates clear mechanisms to encourage participation between elections. Practice follows the same line. In Brazil, in 2013, 85.1% of Latinobarómetro respondents said they had never reached out to a politician.

In the dissertation’s view, it is this model, applied in everyday life, that produces the fragmentation. This explains the appeal of those who promise that “the people will be heard again.” One of these promises is digital democracy, in which everyone would give their opinion over the internet. Does it fix the problem? That is the subject of the next chapter.
